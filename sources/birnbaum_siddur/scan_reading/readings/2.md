# Printed page 2 (English; scan 27, leaf 26) — faces 1

No running head: this is the first opening of the body, and the book sets running heads
from page 3 onward. The page number `2` is printed, centred, at the foot.

Read off the scan, page and rubric at a time, as page 1 was.

## Heading (centred, roman, letterspaced caps)

    MORNING PRAYER FOR CHILDREN

The facing Hebrew page heads the section שַׁחֲרִית לִילָדִים. The two headings are in
different languages — unlike the Amidah, where SHEMONEH ESREH stands in English on both
sides of the opening.

## The page

Four rubrics, each governing what follows it, each word for word identical to the rubric
on the facing Hebrew page. **They are set in italic here and in roman there.** That is
worth stating plainly, because it inverts within a single rubric: see the third one below.

### Rubric (italic, centred)

    Upon awakening in the morning:

[Modeh Ani]

    I render thanks to thee, everlasting King, who hast mercifully restored my soul
    within me; thy faithfulness is great.

### Rubric (italic, centred)

    When washing the hands:

[al netilat yadayim]

    Blessed art thou, Lord our God, King of the universe, who hast sanctified us with
    thy commandments, and commanded us concerning the washing of the hands.

### Rubric (italic, centred)

    When putting on the arba kanfoth:

**`arba kanfoth` is NOT distinguished from the rubric around it here.** The rubric is
italic and so is the transliterated term, so the two are indistinguishable on this page.
On the facing Hebrew page the rubric is roman and the same term is italic, so there it
stands out. Verified at 5x on both pages, side by side, because the first reading of this
page got it backwards.

The intent is the same on both sides — a transliterated Hebrew term, marked as foreign —
and only the realisation differs, because a foreign term set in italic inside an already
italic rubric has nowhere to go. That is a typographic consequence, not two different
encodings: both projects mark it as foreign and let the rendering decide.

[al mitzvat tzitzit]

    Blessed art thou, Lord our God, King of the universe, who hast sanctified us with
    thy commandments, and commanded us concerning the precept of tsitsith.

Birnbaum romanises `tsitsith` here and `arba kanfoth` above; neither is in italic in
this paragraph, only in the rubric.

### Rubric (italic, centred)

    When dressed:

This rubric governs **two** paragraphs, as it does on the Hebrew page.

[Torah tziva — one run-on paragraph of continuous prose, holding nine distinct texts with
no visual break of any kind between them]

    The Torah which Moses handed down to us is the heritage of the community of Jacob.
    May blessings rest on my head. Hear, my son, your father’s instruction, and reject
    not your mother’s teaching. The Torah shall be my trust, and the Almighty my help.
    God is a faithful King. Hear, O Israel, the Lord is our God, the Lord is One.
    Blessed be the name of his glorious majesty forever and ever. You who cling to the
    Lord are all alive today. For thy salvation I hope, O Lord.

Nine sentences for the Hebrew page's nine texts, in the same order, one for one. That
correspondence is the only thing there is to align on: nothing in either column's layout
separates them.

[the abridged Elohai Netzor — a second indented paragraph under the same rubric, with no
rubric of its own]

    My God, guard my tongue from evil, and my lips from speaking falsehood. Open my
    heart to thy Torah, that my soul may follow thy commands. May the words of my mouth
    and the meditation of my heart be pleasing before thee, O Lord, my Stronghold and my
    Redeemer.

Three sentences. The same book's page 96 gives a much longer English meditation after the
Shemoneh Esreh — the clauses about those who insult me and those who plan evil, the four
"do it for the sake of" clauses and the closing peace formula are all absent here.
Different words, so a different text and its own URN.

`sal-vation`, `speak-ing` and `medi-tation` are broken across lines by the compositor;
they are not hyphenated words.

## Footnote apparatus — there is none

Page 2 carries no rule and no note. The opening's single footnote is printed at the foot
of the **Hebrew** page 1, keyed by an unvocalized Hebrew lemma. This is the first opening
in the conversion where the apparatus sits under the Hebrew rather than running across
into the English, and it is worth recording as evidence for the deferred apparatus work.

## Checked against the transcription

The reading was compared afterwards, word for word, against the proofread en.wikisource
transcription of this leaf in `en/text/027.txt`. 185 words, **one difference**, and the
scan settles it for the print:

> `falsehood.` against the transcription's `falsehood,`

Verified at 7x: the print sets a full stop, and `Open` that follows it is capitalised.
The facing Hebrew page agrees — it closes מִרְמָה with a full stop too, where the longer
page-95 meditation has a comma there because the sentence runs on. So the transcription
has punctuated this abridged text as though it were the long one.

That is a check on the reading and not its source — and it is a weaker check than the
Hebrew one, because there is no independent English witness to this print: en.wikisource
transcribes the same scan. What it cannot check at all is what it does not record — the transcription
carries no styling for the rubrics, and it attaches the opening's footnote to this page
rather than to the Hebrew one where the print puts it.
