# Printed page 17 (Hebrew; scan 42, leaf 41)

Running head `ברכות השחר`, page number `17` at the top outer corner. Closes mid-sentence,
in the Yehi Ratzon that finishes on page 19.

## The two-column blessing, and which column comes first

This is the page the `opensiddur:person/gender` setting was added for, and the page settles
how it is encoded.

After `שֶׁלֹּא עָשַֽׂנִי עָֽבֶד`, set full measure, the page breaks into two columns under two
rubrics:

    (right column)  Men say:
    (left column)   Women say:

English roman, as every rubric in this unit is on the Hebrew side.

**"Men say:" stands over the right column.** On a Hebrew page the right column is read
first, so the men's blessing is first in reading order and the women's second. The skill's
rule — order by reading order, rightmost first on a Hebrew page — decides it, and the
columns must not be ordered by their position on the printed page.

**These columns are exclusive alternatives, and are not what Adon Olam does.** On page 11
the two columns were the two halves of one line and both are read. Here only one is read,
and which one depends on who is saying it. Same printed shape, opposite meaning — which is
why column geometry is never evidence by itself.

So: two `j:conditional` blocks on `opensiddur:person/gender`, men's first, each carrying
the rubric the print sets over it. The setting has no default, so a compile that does not
say who is praying keeps both columns with the rubrics that say who each is for, which is
what the printed page does too.

## The rest of the page

The sequence of one-line morning blessings — פוקח עורים through המעביר שנה — each set full
measure, one to a line, with no rubric between them. Then the Yehi Ratzon beginning
שֶׁתַּרְגִּילֵֽנוּ, which runs over the page turn.

Note `וְאַל תַּשְׁלֶט־בָּֽנוּ` with a maqqef, and semicolons rather than commas dividing the
clauses of the Yehi Ratzon — checked, after pages 9 and 11 turned up maqqefs read in both
directions.

## Footnote apparatus (bottom of the page, below a rule)

Three notes, and **the first of them is where the gendered split is explained**: it gives
Menaḥoth 43b as the source of `שלא עשני אשה` and the two blessings before it, and says men
thank God for the privilege of performing precepts incumbent only on male Israelites. A
second note says `שעשני כרצונו` is mentioned by David Abudarham in the fourteenth century
as a recently introduced blessing to be recited by women. A third traces `הנותן ליעף כח`
to Maḥzor Vitry rather than to the Talmud.

**The reason for the split is in the apparatus, and the rubrics carry only "Men say" and
"Women say".** That is the same shape as the tefillin restriction on page 6: the practice
is stated in a rubric, the reason for it in a note. The encoding follows the rubric and
leaves the reason where he put it.

## The comparison

Two differences in 163 words — the cleanest page of the unit so far, and the first with no
whitespace or consonantal difference at all. One is a meteg, settled through
`OPEN_QUESTIONS.md`. One is `כָּל צָרְכִּי`, and it is **not** the qamats-qatan rule,
though this file once said it was: the edition writes `כׇל`, dropping the **dagesh** as
well as reading the vowel as qatan, so two things differ and the rule settles neither.
Checked at 25×: the kaf carries a plain dagesh. Same class as printed page 15's
`רִבּוֹן כָּל`.

This was found by `compare --settle-qamats-qatan`, which applies the rule mechanically and
refuses any difference the rule does not wholly cover.
