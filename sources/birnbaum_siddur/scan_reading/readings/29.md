**A citation can name more than one place**, and then the places are parted by a semicolon:
`שמות ל, לד–לו; ל ז–ח` gives two ranges in one chapter, the second without repeating the
comma; the Talmud citation names two works. As everywhere on the Hebrew side, chapter and
verse take a comma and a range takes an en dash — and these are set in the Hebrew column,
in small unpointed type, and so are recorded here rather than in `hebrew/29.txt`.

## The comparison

Nine differences over 186 words, all settled for the print.

**Four are qamats qatan**, settled by rule.

**One is substantive**: the print reads `פְּרַס בְּשַׁחֲרִית` where the edition sets
`בַּשַּׁחֲרִית`. Checked at 14×: the bet carries a shva — two dots stacked — and not a
pataḥ, and the shin carries no dagesh. Two different words, not two pointings of one.

**Four are the combined shin-dot/holam, and this page is where that decision earns its
keep.** The print sets `שלש` twice and `ושלשה` once with a **single dot** between the lamed
and the shin — one mark standing for both the lamed's holam and the shin's dot, the two
positions being the same point on the page. The edition's editors saw the one dot and had to
decide what it was, and **they decided differently within eight words**:

| the print | the edition | what it dropped |
|---|---|---|
| `שלש` (twice) | `שְׁלֹש` | the final shin's dot |
| `ושלשה` | `וּשְׁלשָׁה` | the lamed's holam |

Neither reading survives on its own — `שְׁלֹשׁ` needs a holam *and* a shin dot, or it is
not that word — so the inconsistency is itself the proof that the printed dot is doing
double duty. The reading writes both marks in both places.

`משֶׁה` in the Atah Hu is the same convention with mem and shin, and the edition drops the
holam there as it does in `וּשְׁלשָׁה`.
