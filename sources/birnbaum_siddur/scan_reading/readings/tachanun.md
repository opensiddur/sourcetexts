# Tachanun, Half Kaddish, and El Erekh Appayim — printed 103–118 (IA n127–n142)

All sixteen scan images were read directly. The cached Wikisource Hebrew foundation text was used as a transcription aid, then collated against the scan. Its modern rubrics and changed readings are not substituted for the 1949 edition.

## Encoding decisions

- Short Tachanun and Monday/Thursday Tachanun share their opening Psalm 6 passage and closing petitions. Psalm 6 begins at verse 2; its superscription is not printed. The two biblical readings (II Samuel 24:14 and Psalm 6:2–11) retain biblical URNs. Mixed biblical/liturgical paragraphs have siddur URNs, with individual biblical sources identified. Other supplications have prayer URNs.
- Tachanun, the following Half Kaddish, and El Erekh Appayim are three separate service units. The omission calendars live only in the transcluding units. Omitting Tachanun does not omit Kaddish. The Half Kaddish reuses the existing text and its Ten Days addition; only the second l’ella is conditional, without duplicating the print’s parentheses.
- The short and long calendars are mutually exclusive by weekday. The printed El Erekh Appayim calendar differs from Tachanun’s: most of Nisan, for example, omits Tachanun but still permits El Erekh Appayim on Mondays/Thursdays.
- As clarified by the project editor, “second day after Sukkoth” is 24 Tishrei in Israel / 25 Tishrei in the Diaspora. These are provisionally treated as resumption dates, pending clarification of the inclusive boundary. The later custom of resuming on 2 Marheshvan, noted by the editor and reflected in Wikisource’s changed rubrics, is documented here rather than substituted for Birnbaum’s printed calendar.
- Physical page breaks within reused passages are supplied by their occurrence wrappers; breaks inside the long supplications remain in the prayer text.
- The incomplete closing quotation on English 104 is completed in the shared encoded reading from its repetition on 114. The diplomatic page below leaves it incomplete. Printed citation numbers restart each page; the apparatus stores the citations once and the renderer renumbers them.
- The scan’s unusual second “שמע מלכנו ותפלתנו” on 111 and “כי כך דרכך” are retained. The printed English “hide not thy face from us” in El Erekh Appayim is retained alongside Hebrew “אל באפך תוכיחנו”.

### תחנון — commentary

(“petition”) is recited in a sitting posture known as נפילת אפים (“falling on the face”), which is a modified form of the complete prostration with the face to the ground practised in the early days of the Talmud (Megillah 22b). This custom originates from Moses, who “fell down before the Lord” (Deuteronomy 9:18), and Joshua, who “fell on the earth upon his face before the ark of the Lord” (Joshua 7:6). Hence, נפילת אפים is performed only where there is a Sefer Torah. It consists of merely resting the head on the arm. During the morning service, when the tefillin are on the left arm, the right arm is used; at the Minḥah service, however, the left arm is used. Since the verse ויאמר דוד contains the phrase נפלה נא (“let us fall”) it precedes נפילת אפים, the falling posture assumed during the Taḥanun prayer.

### גד — commentary

was the name of the prophet who offered to David a choice of punishments, coming directly from God or through the agency of man. David replied that he preferred to be punished by the gracious God rather than by man.

### והוא רחום — commentary

was composed, according to legend, soon after the destruction of the second Temple. It is suggested, however, that it was written during the persecutions of the seventh century. It has been said that whoever can read this long prayer without emotion has lost all feeling for what is great and noble. The soul of an entire people utters these elegies and supplications, and gives voice to its woe of a thousand years.
