# Printed page 11 (Hebrew; scan 36, leaf 35)

Running head `ברכות השחר`, page number `11` at the top outer corner.

Opens mid-verse, finishing Exodus 13:16 from page 9. Closes mid-poem, in the fifth line of
Yigdal, which finishes on page 13.

## Three things on this page

1. The tail of the tefillin parashiyoth.
2. **Adon Olam**, complete, ten lines.
3. **Yigdal**, lines one to five.

## The poems are set in two columns, and the columns are halves of one line

This is a layout the conversion has not met before, and it is not the Amidah's two-column
blocks. There the columns were alternatives — one reading or the other — or two things said
at once. Here each printed row is **one line of the poem, cut in half**: the first
hemistich in the right column, the second in the left, reading right to left as the page
does.

**Birnbaum's own footnote settles it**, which is why this is a finding and not a guess. He
says Adon Olam "is composed of ten lines, each of which consists of twelve syllables. A
single rhyme runs through it," and that Yigdal has "thirteen lines, each of which consists
of sixteen syllables." Ten rows are printed for Adon Olam and thirteen for Yigdal. So the
rows are the lines, and the columns are a setting of each line rather than a structure of
their own.

That decides the encoding: a `tei:lg` of ten `tei:l`, each `tei:l` holding both halves.
Splitting on the printed column would produce twenty lines and contradict the book's own
statement about itself.

**No Hebrew heading for either poem.** They begin directly, the first word of each doing
the work.

**Page 12 has now been read, and it does head them** — ADON OLAM and YIGDAL, centred roman
caps. So this is the sharpest form of the asymmetry in the unit: not a heading in two
languages, as pages 3/4 and 5/6 have, but a heading on one side and none at all on the
other.

Page 12 also settles the lineation. It sets each line of verse as two stacked lines in a
single column where this page sets two columns side by side — twenty printed lines for ten
lines of verse, pairing one for one. So the structure is the same on both sides and only
the setting differs, which is what the footnote's "ten lines" already said. Ten `tei:l` on
both sides.

## Footnote apparatus (bottom of the page, below a rule)

Two commentary notes, keyed as always by catchword.

**אדון עולם** — attributes the hymn to various poets, particularly Solomon ibn Gabirol,
eleventh-century Spain; part of the morning service since the fifteenth century; the ten
lines and twelve syllables quoted above.

**יגדל** — a summary of Maimonides' thirteen principles from his commentary on the Mishnah
(Sanhedrin 10:1), composed by Daniel ben Judah of Rome, fifteenth century; thirteen lines
of sixteen syllables.

The second **runs across the opening** and finishes at the foot of page 12.

**The יגדל note records a variant reading** in the poem's fifth line, saying it "brings out
the full meaning of Maimonides' fifth principle." That is Birnbaum discussing a variant of
the text he is printing, in his own apparatus — worth noting because it is the first place
in this unit where the commentary is text-critical rather than explanatory, and because the
line it discusses is on this page.
