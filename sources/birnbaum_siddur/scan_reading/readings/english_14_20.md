# Printed pages 14, 16, 18, 20 (English; facing 13, 15, 17, 19)

The English side of the next four openings, taken from the en.wikisource transcription of
this scan and checked against the page images, as `english_4_12.md` is. What follows is
what was verified on the image.

## Page 14 (faces 13)

**Yigdal's lines are numbered here and not on the facing page.** This page sets `6.` `7.`
`8.` … against each line of verse; page 13 sets the same lines with no numbers at all.

Independent confirmation of his footnote, which says the poem has thirteen lines: the
English numbers them and stops at thirteen. It also confirms the lineation once more —
each numbered line is set as two stacked lines here, where the Hebrew sets two columns.

**The numbers are not part of the text.** They belong in `@n` on the `tei:l` if anywhere,
on the English side only, and nothing about them changes what either side says.

## Page 16 (faces 15)

The source citations, in the English form:

    Numbers 6:24-26
    Mishnah Peah 1:1; Talmud Shabbath 127a

Colon between chapter and verse, semicolon between two references, book names italic.
Page 15 sets the same three citations with a **comma** where this page has a colon. The
convention now holds across six citations on four openings, and it is a property of the
language the citation is set in rather than of the reference.

## Page 18 (faces 17)

### The two columns are here too, and they are mirrored

    (left column)   Men say:
    (right column)  Women say:

Page 17 puts **Men say:** over the *right* column. This page puts it over the *left*.

That is the inversion the Amidah already records, and this is the clearest instance of it
in the book so far: the Hebrew page sets its columns in Hebrew reading order, right first;
this page mirrors the physical placement so that English reading order, left first, puts
the same blessing first. **The men's blessing is first in reading order on both sides.**

So the encoding is confirmed: the two conditionals go in reading order, men's first, on
both sides, and the mirroring is a fact about the typesetting that the encoding must not
reproduce. Ordering by physical column would put the two sides in opposite orders.

Rubrics italic here, roman there, as throughout this unit.

## Page 20 (faces 19)

**The speaker label is on the Hebrew side only.** Page 19 sets `Reader` in small roman
capitals in the margin against the passage beginning `וּתְנֵֽנוּ הַיּוֹם`. This page sets the
same passage — *Grant us today* — running straight on from the sentence before it, with no
label of any kind.

Worth stating because the Amidah does something different: there the English sets
`Reader:` as its own centred italic line. Here it sets nothing. So the practice is not
uniform across the book, and the English side of this unit simply omits the direction.

An instruction realised on one side and not the other, like the poem headings on pages
11/12 — the same shape of asymmetry, in the opposite direction.
